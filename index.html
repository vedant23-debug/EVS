Drinking water safety

Why water safety matters

Safe drinking water is one of the most basic requirements for staying healthy, yet water that looks, smells and tastes normal is not automatically safe. Many contaminants, including several disease-causing microorganisms and dissolved chemicals, cannot be detected by the human senses at all. This is why routine monitoring and appropriate testing matter more than a visual check — appearance alone cannot confirm that water is safe to drink. Clean storage and careful handling between the source and the point of use are just as important, since water can become contaminated after it has already been treated.

How water can become contaminated

  • Contamination at the source — the source itself (a borewell, tank or supply line) may pick up contaminants from the surrounding soil, runoff or nearby waste.
  • Poor storage — open or poorly sealed storage tanks allow dust, insects and microorganisms to enter over time.
  • Dirty containers — jugs, bottles or dispenser reservoirs that aren't cleaned regularly can reintroduce contamination into otherwise treated water.
  • Poor handling — touching the inside of a container or tap outlet, or using unwashed hands, can transfer microorganisms into the water.
  • Damaged infrastructure — cracked pipes, corroded fittings or broken taps can let contaminants in or let treated water leak out before it's used.
  • Environmental contamination — nearby construction, flooding or poor drainage around a water point can introduce contaminants close to the point of use.

Types of contaminants

Microbiological contaminants

Living organisms that can cause illness, usually linked to contamination from human or animal waste.

Bacteria
Can enter water through contact with sewage or animal waste; monitoring for indicator bacteria is a standard way to flag this risk.
Viruses
Often enter through similar routes as bacteria and can survive in water for extended periods, so they're a reason source protection matters.
Parasites
Organisms such as protozoa can be resistant to some standard disinfection methods, which is part of why layered safeguards (source, treatment and storage) matter.

Chemical contaminants

Dissolved substances that can come from geology, agriculture, industry or plumbing, and that build up gradually rather than causing sudden, visible change.

Arsenic
Occurs naturally in some groundwater from surrounding rock and soil; the World Health Organization's guideline value is 0.01 mg/L, and it is odourless and tasteless, so it can only be confirmed by testing.
Fluoride
Present naturally in many water sources; the WHO guideline value is 1.5 mg/L. Small amounts can support dental health, but higher concentrations over long-term use are a monitoring concern.
Nitrate
Commonly linked to agricultural fertiliser runoff or leaking sewage reaching a water source; the WHO guideline value is 50 mg/L.
Lead
Usually enters water from ageing pipes, solder or fittings rather than the source itself; the WHO guideline value is 0.01 mg/L.

Water quality parameters

Each parameter below tells us something useful, but none of them can prove on its own that water is completely safe to drink. Select a parameter to expand it.

pH

What it measures: how acidic or alkaline the water is.

Why it matters: pH affects how corrosive water is to pipes and how well disinfection works. The WHO has not set a health-based guideline value for pH, though many standards recommend a working range of roughly 6.5–8.5 for taste and system performance.

What it doesn't tell us: a "normal" pH reading says nothing about whether harmful bacteria or specific chemical contaminants are present.

Turbidity

What it measures: how cloudy the water is, caused by suspended particles.

Why it matters: high turbidity can shield microorganisms from disinfection and often signals a problem upstream, such as disturbed sediment or a treatment issue.

What it doesn't tell us: clear water can still carry dissolved chemical contaminants or pathogens that don't create visible cloudiness.

TDS (Total Dissolved Solids)

What it measures: the combined concentration of dissolved minerals and salts in the water.

Why it matters: very high TDS can affect taste and may hint at a source or treatment issue worth investigating. The WHO has not proposed a health-based guideline value for TDS.

What it doesn't tell us: TDS is a general measure — it does not identify which specific substances are dissolved, so a low reading does not rule out a specific contaminant of concern.

Hardness

What it measures: the concentration of dissolved calcium and magnesium.

Why it matters: hardness mainly affects taste, scaling in pipes and how well soap lathers; it's largely an aesthetic and operational parameter rather than a direct health measure.

What it doesn't tell us: hardness has no bearing on whether the water is microbiologically safe.

Microbiological indicators

What it measures: the presence of indicator organisms, most commonly E. coli or other coliform bacteria, used as a proxy for faecal contamination.

Why it matters: because testing for every possible pathogen individually isn't practical, indicator organisms give a reliable early warning that contamination from waste may be present.

What it doesn't tell us: a clean indicator result reduces risk but does not guarantee the complete absence of every possible chemical or biological contaminant.

Safe water storage and handling

  • Keep storage containers and dispenser reservoirs clean and covered.
  • Keep drinking water covered between the source and the point of use.
  • Avoid touching drinking-water contact surfaces, such as tap outlets or the inside of lids, unnecessarily.
  • Wash hands before handling containers, filters or dispensers.
  • Keep the surrounding area of a water point clean and free of standing water or waste.
  • Seek appropriate testing when there is a specific concern, rather than relying on taste or appearance.

What to do if you notice a problem

  1. Notice a problem
  2. Identify the water point
  3. Report the issue
  4. Avoid making assumptions based only on appearance or taste
  5. Seek appropriate testing or authority assistance when necessary

About WaterSafe Campus

Project
WaterSafe Campus
Topic
Drinking water safety awareness through technology
Purpose
To demonstrate how digital technology can connect drinking-water awareness with reporting of problems at campus water points.

Problem

Problems at campus drinking-water points — broken taps, unclean surroundings, unusual taste or smell — often go unreported because there's no quick, obvious way to flag them.

Solution

A simple QR-linked reporting page at each water point, paired with clear educational content, so noticing a problem and reporting it takes under a minute.

Technology used

HTML5, CSS3 and vanilla JavaScript, with browser localStorage standing in for a real backend in this prototype.

Expected impact

Faster awareness of water-point issues, and a more informed student body when it comes to basic drinking-water safety concepts.

Developed as an Environmental Studies academic prototype.
